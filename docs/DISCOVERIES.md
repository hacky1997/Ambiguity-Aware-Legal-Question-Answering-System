# Discovery Log

Things this project learned by checking rather than by thinking.

The decision records next door explain what was decided and why. This file records the findings that forced those decisions, including the ones that were uncomfortable. Several entries are corrections of things confidently asserted and then found to be wrong. Those are kept deliberately, because a project that only records its successes is not telling you how carefully it was built.

Each entry says what was believed, what was found, how it was found, and what it changed.

---

## F1. There is no official mapping between the old and new criminal codes

**Believed.** That the government published a table mapping every provision of the old penal code to its equivalent in the new one, and that this table could be treated as ground truth.

**Found.** India Code, the government's own repository, hosts both the old and the new codes, and neither redirects to the other. It provides a provision-by-provision comparison. That comparison is generated by text similarity rather than issued as an official legal correspondence. Comparison charts published for human readers exist. A machine-readable government correspondence does not.

Practitioner tables, publisher concordances and commercial converters all exist independently, and they do not fully agree with one another, including on whether a mapping is to a section or to a specific sub-section.

**How.** Searching for the official mapping, then reading what the sources actually claimed about themselves rather than what they were assumed to claim.

**Changed.** The correspondence file moved from authoritative source to reviewed working reference. Disputed became a first-class category rather than an error state. See D3.

---

## F2. A machine-readable statute layer exists, and it publishes computed cross-code mappings

**Believed.** That the correspondence file would have to be built by hand, at a cost of somewhere between one hundred and fifty and three hundred hours of qualified legal time.

**Found.** A statute layer serving sections in structured form, requiring no credentials and no token. Markdown at a per-section path, Akoma Ntoso XML at another, and bulk datasets published under CC BY 4.0. The publisher asks that the HTML not be crawled and the structured endpoints be used instead.

It also publishes computed cross-code mappings, one page per provision of the old code, giving the closest provision of the new code by text similarity with a score, ranked next-closest matches with their own scores, both full texts side by side, repeal status, and an explicit no-close-match verdict where the law genuinely changed rather than a guessed successor. Companion mappings exist for procedure and evidence, not only the penal code.

The publisher is explicit that a high score means the words are alike, not that a court has held the provisions equivalent.

**How.** Being asked directly why public sources had not been checked, and then checking them properly. The challenge was correct and the earlier position was not.

**Changed.** Hand annotation was eliminated for correspondence, split candidates, no-successor candidates and most of the vocabulary. The estimate of one hundred and fifty to three hundred hours was withdrawn. See D4.

## F3. Text similarity does not measure whether the punishment changed

This is the single most important finding in the project.

**Believed.** That a high similarity score between an old provision and its successor meant the provision carried across unchanged, and could therefore be answered on a stated assumption without asking the user for a date.

**Found.** IPC 336 maps to BNS 125 with a similarity score of 95 per cent and the label "text carried over almost unchanged".

Reading the two texts side by side:

- The maximum fine moves from 250 rupees to 2,500 rupees.
- A new clause is added for where hurt is caused, carrying up to six months or 5,000 rupees.
- A second new clause is added for where grievous hurt is caused, carrying up to three years or 10,000 rupees.

So a provision scored ninety-five per cent similar and labelled almost unchanged has a tenfold increase in the base fine and two new aggravated sub-offences carrying up to three years of imprisonment.

Running the sentencing extractor over both texts gives maximum imprisonment moving from three months to thirty-six, maximum fine from 250 to 10,000, and sentencing limbs from one to three.

**How.** Fetching a single provision page while checking whether the published mapping could be used directly, and reading the two texts rather than trusting the score.

**Changed.** Everything about how materiality is decided. Similarity is a correspondence signal and answers which provision is the successor. It is not a materiality signal and cannot answer whether the punishment changed. The two questions need two independent signals, and their disagreement is not noise but the most valuable output the pipeline produces. See D5.

Under the finding rules, this provision requires asking the user for a date. Similarity alone would have routed it to answering on an assumption, which would have put a three-month figure in front of someone facing a three-year exposure.

## F4. Published sources disagree about how many provisions the old code contained

**Found.** One analysis works from 553 provisions of the old code, reporting 315 carried over almost unchanged, 161 close with changes, 25 distant, and 52 with no close match. Other sources describe the old code as having 511 sections.

The difference appears to be a provision-versus-section counting question, but it was not resolved.

**Changed.** It became an explicit build task: resolve the counting definition once, record the resolution, and use one definition throughout. More usefully, it is a signal about the domain. If independent sources disagree on the basic count, they will certainly disagree on individual correspondences, which is exactly why the disputed category exists.

## F5. The judgment corpus is public, large, and requires no account

**Found.** Supreme Court and High Court judgments are published as open data under a licence permitting reuse with attribution, downloadable without an account, with structured metadata published alongside the documents. The buckets are hosted in a region close to where the work is being done.

**Changed.** Corpus assembly stopped being a problem. It also produced a practical rule: read the structured metadata first and filter before pulling a single document, because pulling documents first is how a project ends up with hundreds of gigabytes and no plan.

## F6. My own number parser had a bug that the test suite found immediately

**Believed.** That the word-to-number parser handled digit forms correctly.

**Found.** The string representing one hundred thousand in Indian digit grouping parsed as 1. Splitting on commas produced three separate digit tokens which were then summed.

**How.** A test written at the same time as the parser, on a value chosen because Indian numbering groups differently from Western numbering.

**Changed.** The fix was small. The lesson was not. This is precisely the class of bug that produces a confidently wrong fine amount in a legal answer, it was invisible on inspection, and it was caught only because the test used a realistic value rather than a convenient one.

It also justified a requirement: property-based testing on the number parser specifically, because the space of number phrasings in statutes is larger than anyone will enumerate by hand.

## F7. Data protection rules are in force and phased

**Found.** India's data protection rules were notified in November 2025, bringing the Act into force in phases across a defined implementation period, with substantial penalties for non-compliance.

**Changed.** The regime is named as applicable rather than assumed away. It also produced the cheapest compliance posture and the recommended one: store no personal data at all. Strip before logging, keep no user records beyond what authentication requires, define retention accordingly. Obligations that do not attach cannot be breached.

Exact dates and specific obligations were not interpreted, and are flagged for a qualified adviser.

## F8. The chosen orchestration framework has broken across minor versions

**Found.** The graph framework already in use has churned in ways its release notes understate. The interrupt primitive has moved more than once. Checkpoint formats changed enough that programs serialised against one minor version needed migration to work on the next. State channel semantics shifted in ways the changelog describes as minor and users describe as breaking.

**Changed.** Pin the version exactly, and read the changelog before any upgrade. This is the most likely thing to break the build later, and it is entirely preventable.

## F9. Public embedding leaderboards do not predict performance on a specific corpus

**Found.** Several current comparisons make the same point independently: public benchmarks are mostly single-domain retrieval on public datasets and will not reflect a particular corpus, chunk size, language mix or cost ceiling. One reported that on a ten thousand document benchmark, the top-ranked model on the public leaderboard was not the best on their own corpus once price was included.

The current shortlist is real and it moves. Hosted options lead on retrieval quality and on all-round performance, open-weight options lead the open leaderboards, and a truncated large model is repeatedly named as the value choice because dimension reduction cuts vector storage substantially for a small quality drop.

**Changed.** No embedding choice is final until it has been scored on this corpus by this project's own harness. Leaderboards produce a shortlist of two. The harness produces the answer. And a cheaper embedder with a reranker frequently beats an expensive embedder alone at lower total cost, so the reranker gets budgeted before the embedder gets upgraded.

## F10. Agentic development tools carry a specific risk to irreplaceable files

**Found.** Reviews of current agent-first development environments report rate limit instability, opaque credit accounting, and benchmark scores that do not lead the field. More seriously, there are reports of prompt injection and file exfiltration risk, and at least one incident of destructive file deletion through command execution in a high-autonomy mode.

**Changed.** Two artifacts in this project represent human judgement rather than computation and cannot be regenerated: the adjudication dispositions and the hand-checked rows of the golden set. They are now declared never writable by an automated agent, enforced by a pre-commit hook rather than by convention.

It also changed the framing of tool choice. The tool matters less than the structure around it. The evaluation harness is the real protection, because an agent cannot argue its way past a gate that exits non-zero.

## F11. The estimate for building the correspondence file was wrong twice, in opposite directions

**Kept deliberately.**

The first estimate was a weekend, and around three hundred and fifty to three hundred and eighty rows. That was a guess stated with unearned confidence, and it was too low.

The second estimate was one hundred and fifty to three hundred hours of qualified legal time, presented alongside a recommendation to find a collaborator. That was too high, because public sources had not been checked properly.

The actual answer is that most of it derives automatically, and the human work is confined to a disagreement set whose size is not yet known and will be measured by running the pipeline.

**Changed.** A working rule for this project: an estimate given without checking the sources is not an estimate. The size of the adjudication queue, once the pipeline runs over the full old code, will say more about whether this project holds together than any document written for it.

## F12. The golden set needs roughly four times as many items as a first pass would produce

**Found.** The evaluation harness suppresses any rate computed on fewer than twenty items, reporting insufficient data rather than a number. There are sixteen findings. A golden set that can report per-finding correctness therefore needs at least twenty items per finding that can realistically fire.

That puts the floor around three hundred items, not the eighty or so that writing a few examples per scenario would naturally produce.

**How.** Tracing the harness's own suppression rule against the number of findings, during a walkthrough of the system as an implementer rather than a reviewer.

**Changed.** Golden set coverage per finding became a build requirement rather than an aspiration, and the harness now reports which findings fall below the floor. It also materially changed the estimate of work remaining, which is exactly the kind of thing that should be discovered before writing code rather than after.
